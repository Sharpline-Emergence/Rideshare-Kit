GST/HST for drivers

Do Uber and Lyft drivers need a GST number in Canada?

By Peter, BC Uber/Lyft driver · Updated November 2026

Yes, from your very first fare. The $30,000 small-supplier exemption that lets most new businesses skip GST doesn’t apply to rideshare. If you drive for Uber or Lyft in Canada, even one night a week, you have to register for a GST/HST account, report the tax on your fares and file returns with the CRA.

This catches a lot of new drivers. Most people have heard “you don’t need a GST number until you make $30,000,” and for most small businesses that’s true. Rideshare is different on purpose. Since July 1, 2017, the CRA has treated rideshare the same as a taxi business, and taxis have always had to register from day one.

Why the $30,000 rule doesn’t apply to you

The federal government changed the definition of “taxi business” in 2017 to include rides arranged through an app. Taxi businesses are excluded from the small-supplier exemption, so rideshare drivers are too. It doesn’t matter how little you earn or whether driving is a side gig.

Two things the rule doesn’t automatically cover:

  • Food delivery (Uber Eats, DoorDash, Skip) follows the normal rules, so the $30,000 threshold still applies to that income. If you do both, have an accountant confirm how to report each.
  • Quebec works differently. Uber collects and remits GST and QST for drivers there, and you deal with Revenu Québec. This guide covers the rest of Canada.

How to register (it takes about 10 minutes)

  1. Go to the CRA’s Business Registration Online service. You can register as a sole proprietor using your SIN. No company needed.
  2. Choose GST/HST as the program account. Use your real start date: your registration takes effect the day you started driving, and you have 30 days from then to apply.
  3. You’ll get a 15-character number ending in RT0001 right away.
  4. Add the number to your Uber and Lyft driver profiles so it shows on your rides.

Already driving without a number? Register now and use your real start date. The CRA will expect returns back to that date. It’s far easier to fix this yourself than to wait for the CRA to find it, and Uber and Lyft now report driver earnings to the CRA.

How much GST you actually owe

Rideshare fares are tax-included. You don’t add GST on top; part of every fare already is GST. The rate depends on the province where the trip starts:

  • 5% GST in BC, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and the territories
  • 13% HST in Ontario
  • 14–15% HST in the Atlantic provinces

To find the GST inside a BC fare, divide the fare by 1.05:

You don’t have to work this out trip by trip. Uber shows the GST/HST charged on your rides on your weekly statements and on your monthly and annual tax summaries. Check Lyft’s earnings summaries for the same figures. That money isn’t a bonus. It’s the CRA’s, so set it aside as it comes in.

The good news: you get GST back on your costs

As a registrant, you can claim back the GST you pay on business expenses. These are called input tax credits. For drivers that usually means the business share of:

  • fuel, oil changes and repairs
  • car washes and cleaning
  • your phone and data plan
  • the GST charged on the apps’ service fees
  • a vehicle lease, or the vehicle itself

What you send the CRA is the GST you collected minus those credits. Keep every receipt; the GST amount has to be on it.

The quick method: often simpler, sometimes cheaper

Instead of tracking GST on every receipt, small businesses can elect the quick method. In BC and the other GST-only provinces, a service business remits 3.6% of its GST-included fares, with a 1% credit on the first $30,000. The catch: you can’t claim input tax credits on everyday costs like fuel and repairs (you still can on a vehicle purchase).

Same BC driver: $30,000 in fares, $4,200 in costs (GST included)Owing
Regular method: $1,428.57 collected − $200.00 in credits$1,228.57
Quick method: 3.6% × $30,000 − 1% × $30,000$780.00

For many part-time drivers the quick method comes out lower. If your costs are high relative to your fares, it may not. Run both before you elect, and make the choice with your accountant. You elect online in My Business Account or with form GST74.

When it’s due

Most drivers are assigned annual filing. For an individual with a December 31 year-end:

  • April 30: pay the GST you owe for last year
  • June 15: file the GST/HST return

Pay by April 30 even though the return isn’t due until June. Interest starts on unpaid GST after April 30. And if you owe $3,000 or more a year, the CRA will ask for quarterly instalments.

Quiet year? Still file. A return is due for every reporting period you’re registered for, even one where you hardly drove. A nil return takes five minutes online. A missed one can mean penalties.

The three mistakes I see most

  1. Spending the GST. It arrives mixed in with your pay, so it feels like income. Move it to a separate savings account each week.
  2. Reporting income net of fees. On your income tax return, report gross fares and deduct the apps’ fees as an expense. Uber and Lyft report your gross earnings to the CRA, so the numbers should line up.
  3. Losing receipts. No receipt, no input tax credit. Snap a photo at the pump.

Quick answers

I only drive a few hours a week. Do I really need to register?

Yes. For rideshare there’s no minimum. The registration rule applies from your first paid ride.

Does Uber pay the GST to the CRA for me?

Outside Quebec, no. The GST/HST is charged to riders on your behalf and shows on your statements, but reporting it and paying the CRA is your job.

I drive for both Uber and Lyft. Do I need two numbers?

No. One GST/HST number covers all your driving. Give the same number to both apps and combine both apps’ amounts on one return.

I drive a taxi or limo, not rideshare. Does this apply?

Yes. Self-employed taxi drivers have always had to register from day one, and limousine fares are treated the same way.
