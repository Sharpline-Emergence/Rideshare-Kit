Vehicle expenses

What car expenses can Uber and Lyft drivers claim in Canada?

By Peter, BC Uber/Lyft driver · Updated November 2026

You can claim the business share of almost every cost of running your car. Fuel, insurance, repairs, licence and registration, car washes, and lease payments or depreciation all count. The share is your business-use percentage: business kilometres ÷ total kilometres for the year. To prove it, you need a mileage log.

For most drivers the car is the biggest deduction on the return. Done properly, it can cut thousands off your taxable income. Done sloppily, without a log, it’s the first thing the CRA questions.

What you can claim

Multiply each of these by your business-use percentage:

  • Fuel and oil
  • Insurance, including any extra coverage you pay for to drive rideshare
  • Maintenance and repairs: oil changes, brakes, tires, servicing
  • Licence and registration fees
  • Car washes and interior cleaning
  • Lease payments, if you lease
  • Interest on a car loan, if you financed it (interest only, not the loan payments themselves)
  • Depreciation (capital cost allowance), if you own the car

Two costs you can claim in full, not by percentage: parking you pay for while working (say, at the airport or downtown), and supplementary business insurance on the car.

Caps apply. For passenger vehicles, the CRA limits how much lease, loan interest and depreciation you can claim. The limits are updated each year, so check the current figures before you file. The kit has them built in.

What you can’t claim

  • Traffic and parking tickets. Fines are never deductible.
  • The personal share of your costs: groceries, school runs, weekend trips.
  • The CRA’s per-kilometre rate (the “72 cents a km” you may have heard about). Those rates are for employers paying employees. Self-employed drivers claim actual costs.

Which kilometres count as business?

For rideshare, the core of it is your time with the app on: waiting for a request, driving to a pickup and carrying a rider. Personal errands don’t count, even if the app happens to be on. If you regularly drive somewhere busy before you go online, ask an accountant how to treat that stretch.

Uber’s annual tax summary shows your online kilometres, which is a handy cross-check. It isn’t a replacement for your own log, though. The app can’t know your total kilometres, and the percentage needs both numbers.

What your mileage log needs

The CRA expects a record showing, for business driving, the date, where you went, why, and how far, plus your odometer reading at the start and end of the year. For a driver, the practical version is simple:

  1. Note the odometer when you start a shift and when you end it.
  2. Write down your odometer on January 1 and December 31.
  3. If you drive a second car for work, keep a separate log for it.

After one full year, the CRA lets you use a simplified log: track a sample period of at least three months in later years, as long as your business use stays within 10 percentage points of the full year.

Claiming 100%? Only if the car is never used personally, which is rare unless you own a second car. A very high percentage with a thin log is exactly what the CRA looks at.

Other expenses drivers often miss

  • The business share of your phone and data plan
  • The apps’ service fees. Report your gross fares as income, then deduct the fees.
  • Things for riders: water, mints, phone chargers
  • Accounting or tax software fees for your business return

If you’re on the regular GST method, the same receipts also get you back the GST you paid. See Do Uber and Lyft drivers need a GST number?

Where it goes on your return

Vehicle expenses go in Chart A – Motor vehicle expenses on Form T2125, the business income form, and flow to the motor vehicle expenses line. Depreciation is worked out in the capital cost allowance section of the same form.

Quick answers

I started driving mid-year. Can I still claim my car?

Yes. Your percentage covers the part of the year you were in business. Rebuild earlier months from your Uber and Lyft trip records as best you can, then log every shift from now on. A log kept as you go carries more weight than one rebuilt later.

My spouse drives the car too. Does that matter?

Their driving is personal use, so it goes into your total kilometres but not your business kilometres. That’s why the year-start and year-end odometer readings matter.

Can I deduct the full price of my car?

Not in one go. You claim depreciation over several years, at the business-use percentage, and passenger vehicles have a cost cap.
